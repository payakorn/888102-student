The session in four parts

Hands-on 10.3 · roadmap

Part 10.3 found the least-squares line by nudging a slope and an intercept until no nudge helped. This session hands the same job to a library: scikit-learn fits the line in one command, and the rest of the time goes to reading what it found.

A · Ten houses, with the toolCorrelation first; LinearRegression().fit finds the line in one command; the slope and R² are read, a prediction is made, and the residual plot is drawn.
B · A real dataset442 diabetes patients bundled with scikit-learn: which measurement correlates most with disease progression, the BMI line fitted and judged, then a second predictor added.
C · ExercisesFour graded exercises on the two datasets, each with a hint and a solution.
D · Where this leaves usA line predicts a number, judged by R² and bounded by the data it was fitted on.

Three commands LinearRegression().fit(X, y) fits the line from the predictor X and the response y. .intercept_ and .coef_ hold a and b. .predict(new_rows) substitutes a new x; .score(X, y) is R². Everything else on these slides is preparing the data and reading the result.

The ten house sales

Hands-on 10.3 · A · the training data

The two variables area_sqft is the predictor, x. price_1000s is the response, y, in thousands of dollars. Each row is one sale — one point on the scatter two slides from now.

Interpretation Ten rows, two columns. Every row already carries its price — that is what lets a line be fitted from it, the same way every golf day carried its answer in Part 10.1.

Do the two variables move together?

Hands-on 10.3 · A · correlation, before fitting anything

np.corrcoef(x, y) returns a 2×2 table; the off-diagonal entry is r. Checking it first asks whether there is a straight-line relationship worth fitting.

Interpretation r = 0.7621 — positive and fairly strong: bigger houses tend to sell for more, though the relationship is far from perfect. A line is worth fitting.

Fitting the line in one command

Hands-on 10.3 · A · LinearRegression

LinearRegression().fit(X, y) finds a and b by least squares — the same search Part 10.3 ran by nudging, done exactly. X must be a table, so the one predictor is passed as a one-column table, not a plain column.

Interpretation

  • a = 98.25 — the intercept. No house has 0 sq ft, so it has no meaning of its own; it positions the line.
  • b = 0.1098 — the slope. Each extra square foot adds about $110 to the predicted price.
  • The whole of Part 10.3’s search — try a line, measure the total squared miss, nudge, repeat — runs inside this one .fit() call.

The line, drawn over the points

Hands-on 10.3 · A · the same command as for the golf tree’s drawing

matplotlib draws the ten points and the fitted line together; in the browser the drawing is saved as a picture file and the picture is shown, exactly as Part 10.1’s hands-on session drew a tree.

Interpretation The line rises left to right, at the slope just read: 0.1098 thousand dollars per square foot. The points sit close to it but not on it — the vertical gaps are the residuals the next slides measure.

Predicting a 2,000 sq ft house

Hands-on 10.3 · A · substitution

.predict() substitutes a new area into the fitted line. The new house is written as a one-row, one-column table with the same column name as the training data.

Interpretation $317,784 — the same figure Part 10.3 reached by substituting into \(98.25 + 0.1098 \times 2000\) by hand. .predict() does the substitution; the equation is the one already read.

Try it Change 2000 to 500 and to 12000 and run again. Both return a number — nothing stops them — but neither is inside the 1,100–2,450 range the line was fitted on.

Judging the fit — R², MAE, RMSE

Hands-on 10.3 · A · how good is this line?

.score(X, y) is R², the share of the variation in price the line explains. The residuals — actual minus predicted — give two more numbers: the typical size of a miss.

Interpretation

  • R² = 0.5808, matching \(r^2 = 0.7621^2\): area explains about 58% of the variation in price among these ten houses.
  • MAE 31.04, RMSE 36.97 thousand dollars — the line is typically about $31,000 out, with a couple of larger misses pulling the RMSE above the MAE.

The residual plot

Hands-on 10.3 · A · is a straight line the right shape?

Residuals plotted against predicted price. For an adequate model this should look like noise scattered around zero, with no curve and no widening spread.

Interpretation The ten points scatter above and below zero with no rising curve and no widening funnel — noise, not a pattern. A straight line was a reasonable shape to fit here, even though it leaves 42% of the variation unexplained.

A real dataset — 442 patients

Hands-on 10.3 · B · the same commands on real data

Ten houses can be checked by hand. A real dataset cannot, and here the library earns its place. The dataset comes with it — no file to download.

The data 442 diabetes patients, each with ten measurements at the start of a study — age, sex, body mass index (BMI), blood pressure, six blood-serum values — and a progression score measured a year later.

The question Does BMI predict how far the disease progresses, and how much of the variation does it explain? The predictor is BMI; the response is the progression score.

What changes Ten predictors are available, not one. Correlation ranks them before any line is fitted, and a second predictor is added later in the session.

Where the dataset comes from sklearn.datasets bundles several small real datasets: load_diabetes (these patients), load_breast_cancer (569 tumors, used in Part 10.2), load_iris (150 flowers, used in Part 10.1). Each loads in one line and works offline once the library is present.

Loading the patients

Hands-on 10.3 · B · the data as a table

load_diabetes(as_frame=True, scaled=False) returns the ten measurements in their original units and the progression score as the target, kept in one table.

Interpretation 442 rows, 11 columns: ten measurements and the progression score a year later. s1 to s6 are blood-serum values; bmi and bp are body mass index and blood pressure.

Ranking the predictors by correlation

Hands-on 10.3 · B · which measurement moves with progression?

.corr() computes the correlation of every pair of columns; taking the progression column and sorting it ranks the ten measurements.

Interpretation BMI has the strongest correlation, 0.586, with the serum value s5 close behind at 0.566 and blood pressure at 0.441. None is as strong as the 0.762 of the ten houses — real data rarely lines up as tightly as ten hand-picked sales.

Fitting the BMI line

Hands-on 10.3 · B · the same command, a new predictor

LinearRegression().fit(X, y) again — only the columns change. BMI goes in as a one-column table, progression as the target.

Interpretation b = 10.23: each extra unit of BMI goes with about 10 more points of progression. R² = 0.344 — BMI explains about a third of the variation in progression; the other two thirds is everything the line cannot see.

Try it Compare 0.344 with 0.586 ** 2. They match — for one predictor, R² is exactly the square of the correlation, as Part 10.3 stated.

A second predictor

Hands-on 10.3 · B · does blood pressure do better or worse?

The same three lines, with bp in place of bmi, then both together — the same call handles one predictor or several.

Interpretation

  • BP alone explains less (R² 0.1949) than BMI alone (0.344) — exactly the order the correlation table already predicted (0.441 < 0.586).
  • Together they explain more (R² 0.3960) than either alone, but less than the two R²’s added — the two measurements overlap in what they tell the model about a patient.

Exercise 3 — a line on blood pressure alone

Hands-on 10.3 · C · reading R² for a different predictor

Fill the blank with the column name for blood pressure and report R² for that line alone, rounded to four decimals.

The correlation table two slides back listed the column names exactly. Blood pressure’s column is bp.

X_one = patients[["bp"]]
one_line = LinearRegression().fit(X_one, y_prog)
round(one_line.score(X_one, y_prog), 4)

0.1949. 0.441 ** 2 gives the same number: for one predictor, R² is the square of its correlation with the response.

Exercise 4 — two predictors together

Hands-on 10.3 · C · multiple linear regression

Fill the blank so both BMI and blood pressure enter the model, and report R², rounded to four decimals.

patients[["bmi", "______"]] needs a second real column name, in quotes, exactly like "bmi" beside it.

X_two = patients[["bmi", "bp"]]
two_line = LinearRegression().fit(X_two, y_prog)
round(two_line.score(X_two, y_prog), 4)

0.396. Add print(two_line.coef_) above the last line: BMI’s own coefficient changes once blood pressure is also in the equation, because part of what BMI predicted was already explained by blood pressure.

What to keep from the session

Hands-on 10.3 · D · takeaways

Three commands LinearRegression().fit(X, y) fits the line; .intercept_ and .coef_ read it; .predict() substitutes a new x and .score() is R². The least-squares search of Part 10.3 runs inside the first one.

R² is the square of r, for one predictor BMI’s R² of 0.344 is exactly \(0.586^2\). With more than one predictor this stops holding, but R² still means the same thing: the share of the variation explained.

A number always comes back — inside the data or not .predict() answered for 500 sq ft and for 12,000 sq ft exactly as readily as for 2,000. Only the range the line was fitted on says which answer is evidence.
