The golf tree, rebuilt

Hands-on 10.2 · A · picking up from Part 10.1

The fourteen golf days and the tree grown from them, exactly as in the hands-on session of Part 10.1 — one cell, so this session does not depend on the other one still being open.

Interpretation The same tree Part 10.1 grew and drew: four questions deep, seven leaves, one command. The rest of Part A asks how good it is — first on its own days, then on days it has not seen.

The tree’s own days, scored by the library

Hands-on 10.2 · A · confusion_matrix and classification_report

confusion_matrix(actual, predicted, labels=...) counts the four boxes; classification_report prints accuracy, precision, recall and F₁ for every class at once. Both are run first on the tree’s own fourteen days.

Interpretation Every day on its own diagonal, every metric 1.00. This is the training score from Part 10.1, read by the library instead of .score() — it says the tree fits every day it was grown from, and nothing about a day it has not seen.

Watch the convention

Hands-on 10.2 · A · rows and columns

scikit-learn’s rows are actual confusion_matrix(actual, predicted) puts the true class in the rows and the predicted class in the columns — the opposite of the diagram in Part 10.2, which draws predicted in the rows. Both tables hold the same four numbers; only the layout differs.

Why it is written this way here The cell above builds a labeled pandas table from the raw array so the rows and columns say what they are, rather than trusting the position by memory. Reading any confusion matrix starts with checking which axis is which.

Testing on a day it has not seen

Hands-on 10.2 · A · leave-one-out cross-validation

LeaveOneOut() holds back one day at a time; cross_val_predict grows a tree on the other thirteen and predicts the one held back, for every day in turn — fourteen small trees, one prediction each.

Interpretation

  • Every row is now a held-out prediction, not a fitted one: day 4’s tree, for instance, never saw day 4 while it was grown.
  • Some rows disagree with the play column — those are the days the library’s tree gets wrong once it is tested honestly.

The library’s held-out confusion matrix

Hands-on 10.2 · A · the honest score

The same two tools as before, now on the held-out predictions instead of the training ones.

Interpretation 64%, not 100%. Tested honestly, the library’s tree is right on 9 of the 14 days — worse than the 79% of the counting tree in the lecture deck. The two trees ask different questions (Part 10.1’s hands-on session showed the library needs four questions where the counting tree used two), and different questions can generalize differently even when both fit their training days perfectly.

The lesson survives the disagreement Whichever tree is grown, its training score says nothing about its held-out score. That is the one fact both numbers agree on.

Precision, recall and F₁ by name

Hands-on 10.2 · A · one function per metric

classification_report prints every metric at once; precision_score, recall_score and f1_score compute one at a time and need a pos_label — the class counted as positive.

Try it Change pos_label='Yes' to pos_label='No' in all three lines and run again. The numbers change — precision, recall and F₁ are always metrics of one class, and “No” is a harder class to catch here, with only five days.

A real dataset — 569 tumors

Hands-on 10.2 · B · the same tools on real data

Fourteen held-out predictions can be checked by hand. A real dataset needs a proper split and cannot — and here the library’s tools earn their place.

The data 569 breast-tumor images, each with 30 measurements of the cell nuclei — radius, texture, how concave the outline is — given as a mean, a standard error and a worst value.

The question From the measurements, is a tumor malignant or benign? The positive class is malignant — the one a hospital is looking for.

What changes 569 rows are enough for a genuine train/test split: 70% trains the tree, 30% is held back and never touched until the tree predicts it.

Loading and splitting the tumors

Hands-on 10.2 · B · a proper train/test split

load_breast_cancer loads the data; train_test_split(..., stratify=y) shuffles the 569 rows and cuts them 70/30 while keeping the malignant share the same in both parts.

Interpretation 398 tumors to learn from, 148 of them malignant; 171 held back, 64 of them malignant — the same 37% share in both parts. The 171 test tumors’ diagnoses stay hidden from the tree until it predicts them.

Growing and testing the tree

Hands-on 10.2 · B · training accuracy vs. test accuracy

A tree three questions deep is grown on the training tumors only. .score() on the training rows is how well it fits; .score() on the test rows — untouched until now — is how well it predicts.

Interpretation 98.5% on the rows it learned from, 91.2% on the rows it did not — the same gap the golf tree showed, on a dataset too large to check the fourteen-day way.

The test tumors, confusion matrix and report

Hands-on 10.2 · B · reading the mistakes

The two tools from Part A, on the 171 test tumors, with malignant as the positive class read from classification_report’s own row.

Interpretation

  • TP 56, FP 7, FN 8, TN 100 — reading malignant as positive: 56 malignant tumors caught, 8 missed, 7 benign tumors flagged, 100 correctly cleared.
  • Precision 0.89, recall 0.88 — of the tumors the tree calls malignant, 89% really are; of the tumors that really are malignant, 88% are caught.
  • 8 false negatives is the number a hospital watches hardest: 8 malignant tumors this tree would have called benign.

Try it Change max_depth=3 to max_depth=2 and run this cell and the one above again. Training accuracy falls a little; watch whether the test accuracy falls with it.

Five-fold cross-validation

Hands-on 10.2 · B · is 91.2% a lucky split?

One split can be lucky or unlucky. cross_val_score(model, X, y, cv=5) refits the tree five times, each time on a different four-fifths of the tumors, and tests it on the fifth left out.

Interpretation Five scores from 0.894 to 0.947, averaging 0.917 — close to, and a little above, the single 91.2% of the one split. The scores agree with each other, so that one split was not a lucky or unlucky draw.

Exercise 1 — recall with the other class

Hands-on 10.2 · C · pos_label changes the metric

The recall of Yes, held out, was 0.6667. Fill the blank so the same function reports the recall of No instead, rounded to four decimals.

pos_label must match a value in y_golf exactly, including capitalization: "Yes" or "No".

round(recall_score(y_golf, loo_pred, pos_label="No"), 4)

0.6: of the five days golf was not played, the held-out tree correctly says No for three of them.

Exercise 2 — a shallower tumor tree

Hands-on 10.2 · C · depth and F1

The depth-3 tumor tree scored F₁ 0.8819 on the test tumors, with malignant positive. Grow one that may ask only two questions and report its F₁ on the same test tumors, rounded to four decimals.

max_depth is the number of questions on the longest path from the root. The tree of the slides used 3; this one uses one fewer.

shallow = DecisionTreeClassifier(max_depth=2, random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
round(f1_score(y_test, shallow.predict(X_test), pos_label="malignant"), 4)

0.864. Add print(confusion_matrix(y_test, shallow.predict(X_test), labels=["benign","malignant"])) above the last line to see which count moved.

Exercise 3 — cross-validating a deeper tree

Hands-on 10.2 · C · does more depth help under cross-validation?

The depth-3 tree’s five-fold mean was 0.9174. Run the same five-fold cross-validation on a tree with no depth limit and report the mean, rounded to four decimals.

Leaving max_depth out of DecisionTreeClassifier(...) altogether removes the limit. Only random_state=0 needs to be passed.

scores = cross_val_score(DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0), X, y, cv=5)
round(scores.mean(), 4)

About 0.9155–0.917 (five-fold splits shuffle, so this varies slightly run to run) — no real gain over the depth-3 tree’s 0.9174, despite a training accuracy of 100%.

Exercise 4 — fitting against predicting

Hands-on 10.2 · C · the gap the golf tree already showed

Grow a tree with no depth limit on the training tumors and report its test accuracy on X_test, rounded to four decimals.

random_state fixes how ties are broken while growing; use 0, the value used everywhere else on these slides.

full = DecisionTreeClassifier(random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
round(full.score(X_test, y_test), 4)

0.9181. Add print(full.score(X_train, y_train)) above the last line: it prints 1.0 — a tree that fits its training rows perfectly can still predict slightly better, or worse, than a shallower one. The training score alone never says which.

What to keep from the session

Hands-on 10.2 · D · takeaways

Three functions confusion_matrix counts the four boxes; classification_report prints every metric at once; cross_val_score repeats the held-out test and returns every score. precision_score, recall_score and f1_score compute one metric with a chosen pos_label.

Rows are actual, columns are predicted scikit-learn’s own convention is the opposite of the diagram’s — check which axis is which before reading any confusion matrix, in this course or elsewhere.

Training accuracy still proves nothing Both classifiers scored near 100% on their own rows and markedly lower on rows they had not seen. Cross-validation is how that held-out score is trusted rather than guessed at from one split.

Take-home The Chapter 10.2 notebook for Colab carries the same tools on the same tumors, with a graded homework that adds precision/recall trade-offs and a bonus on the iris flowers. Work there keeps; this page forgets when it is reloaded.

Where this leaves us A classifier has been built and judged twice, on fourteen days and on 569 tumors, by the same three functions. The hands-on of Part 10.3 turns from predicting a category to predicting a number.
