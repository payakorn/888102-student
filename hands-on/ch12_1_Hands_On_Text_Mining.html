The thirty reviews

Hands-on 12.1 · A · step 1 · the data

Thirty short reviews of a noodle shop that takes orders through an app and delivers. Each carries the customer’s star rating, 1 to 5, which is kept to one side for now: the text is what this session works on.

Interpretation Thirty rows, two columns — and the column that matters is text. Nothing in it can be summed or averaged yet. Reviews 1 to 15 were rated 4 or 5 stars, 16 to 30 were rated 1 or 2; that is a fact about the data, not something the code has been told.

Text preprocessing

Hands-on 12.1 · A · step 2 · one messy review, cleaned

A review as a customer might type it, with capitals, exclamation marks, an emoticon and trailing dots. .lower() lowercases every letter; re.sub replaces every character that is not a letter, a digit or a space with nothing — the next slide reads its pattern piece by piece.

Interpretation

  • COLD and Delivery are now cold and delivery, the same as in every other review.
  • !!!, :( and … are gone. Where the emoticon stood there are two spaces; the next step splits on spaces, so they do no harm.
  • The words and their order are untouched: preprocessing changes the form of the text, not what it says.

Try it Type a review of your own into messy, with capitals and symbols — "GREAT soup!!! 10/10 :)" — and run again.

Reading the pattern

Hands-on 12.1 · A · what [^a-z0-9\s] means

The first argument of re.sub is a pattern: a short description of the characters to find. Read piece by piece, [^a-z0-9\s] finds one character that is not a letter, a digit or a space.

Piece Meaning
[ ... ] any one character from the list inside
^ (first inside [) not: every character outside the list
a-z any lowercase letter, a to z
0-9 any digit, 0 to 9
\s any space, tab or new line

Interpretation

  • Without the ^, [a-z0-9\s] would find the letters, digits and spaces themselves — and replacing them would leave only the symbols.
  • a-z but not A-Z: a capital letter is “not in the list” and would be removed. That is why the text is lowercased first.
  • Outside the brackets ^ means something else: ^The finds The only at the start of the text.

Tokenization and stop words

Hands-on 12.1 · A · step 3 · cut into words

A CountVectorizer carries an analyzer: the function that lowercases a text, drops the punctuation and cuts it into tokens — the cleaning of the last slide and the cutting, in one call. With stop_words="english" it also removes the common words that carry no meaning of their own. Review 16 is put through both.

Interpretation

  • Ten tokens come out of the sentence: lowercase, the final period gone, one word per token — the figure on the lecture slide, produced by the tool.
  • Five remain after filtering. The, and, were and two are on scikit-learn’s English stop-word list; delivery, took, hours, noodles and cold are the words that say something.
  • Try it: replace text with messy from the slide before, and the analyzer cleans the messy review by itself.

The bag of words

Hands-on 12.1 · A · text becomes a table of counts

fit_transform runs the analyzer over all thirty reviews, collects every distinct word as a column, and counts how many times each word appears in each review. The result is the bag of words: one row per review, one column per word, a number in every cell.

Interpretation

  • 30 rows, 89 columns. Thirty reviews use 89 distinct words once the stop words are gone. Each review is now a row of 89 numbers — data of the kind every earlier chapter worked with.
  • Review 1 has noodles and delicious; review 16 has noodles, delivery and cold; review 30 has noodles, delicious, delivery and late. The order of the words is gone — a bag, not a sentence — but which words were used is kept.

Try it Replace the five column names with counts.columns[:12] to see the first twelve words of the vocabulary, or remove stop_words="english" and run the cell again to watch the column count grow.

The most frequent words

Hands-on 12.1 · A · what the reviews talk about

Summing each column gives the number of times a word appears across all thirty reviews. Sorted and drawn, the top of the list says what the customers write about most.

Interpretation

  • Delivery and price lead with 6 each, then order with 5 and app, food, late, noodles, service with 4. These are the aspects of the shop the customers write about — the parts 12.3 scores one by one.
  • A count says what a review is about, not whether the customer was happy. Delivery appears in good and bad reviews alike; the next slide shows where late appears.

The reviews that use a word

Hands-on 12.1 · A · one column of the bag of words

A column of the bag of words can pick out rows, like any column of a table. counts["late"] > 0 is True for every review that uses late; the matching reviews are shown with their stars.

Interpretation

  • Reviews 24, 27, 29 and 30 use late, and all four were rated 1 or 2 stars. The word alone is a strong hint of a complaint about delivery.
  • This is still counting, not reading. Nothing here knows that late is bad; it happens to appear in bad reviews. 12.2 gives words a score of their own.

Try it Change "late" to "fair", "delivery" or "app". Fair appears only in 4- and 5-star reviews; delivery and app appear in both.

Exercise 2 — the stop-word list

Hands-on 12.1 · B · step 3 with the tool

Without stop words the thirty reviews have 89 distinct words. Fill the blank with the name of the built-in stop-word list, so that the box counts the words that remain.

scikit-learn has one built-in list, and it is named after the language the reviews are written in, in lowercase.

counts is the bag of words. Fill the blank with a word so that the box returns how many times delivery appears in the thirty reviews.

Every column of counts is named after one word, in lowercase. .sum() adds the column down all thirty reviews.

Exercise 4 — keeping the stop words

Hands-on 12.1 · B · what filtering removes

stop_words=None tells CountVectorizer to keep every word. Fill the blank with None and the box counts the distinct words with the stop words kept.

None is Python’s word for “nothing”: no stop-word list. It is written with a capital N and without quotes.

len(CountVectorizer(stop_words=None).fit(reviews["review"]).get_feature_names_out())

117. Against the 89 of Exercise 2, the stop-word list removed 28 distinct words.

What to keep from the session

Hands-on 12.1 · C · takeaways

Clean first .lower() and re.sub put a messy review in one form: lowercase, no symbols, the words unchanged.

Text becomes a table by counting CountVectorizer cleans, cuts, removes stop words and counts: thirty reviews became 30 rows by 89 words.

A count shows the topic Delivery and price are the most frequent words; late appears only in low-star reviews. But no count knows that late is bad.

Take-home The Chapter 12.1 notebook for Colab carries the same steps with a graded homework on a second set of reviews. Work there keeps; this page forgets when it is reloaded.

Where this leaves us Every review is a row of word counts. 12.2 gives each word a score from a lexicon and each review a label: positive, negative or neutral.
