The session in four parts

Hands-on 11 · roadmap

Chapter 9 grew K-means and a hierarchical clustering tree by hand, and computed support, confidence and lift for one rule, all on small toy examples. This session hands the same three jobs to a library, on data too large to work by hand: 150 iris flowers, and twelve grocery transactions.

A · K-means, with the toolThe iris flowers, standardized, clustered with scikit-learn, and checked against the species label they never saw.
B · Hierarchical clustering, with the toolA 15-flower sample, its dendrogram drawn by SciPy, cut into clusters two different numbers of ways.
C · Association rules, with the toolTwelve grocery transactions, every rule between two items, filtered down to the ones worth a business’s attention.
D · ExercisesFour graded exercises across the three jobs, each with a hint and a solution.

Three tools KMeans(n_clusters=k).fit(X) and linkage(X, method="average") do the jobs Chapter 9 built by hand. Association rules have no single scikit-learn class; a five-line support() function, applied to every pair of items, does the same job a library would.

The iris flowers, unlabeled

Hands-on 11 · A · a real dataset, no species column

load_iris bundles 150 flowers with scikit-learn — no download needed. Only the four measurements are kept as flowers; the species is kept separately, as species, and not shown to any clustering method below.

Interpretation 150 rows, four measurements, no target column. Everything K-means and hierarchical clustering find below comes only from these four numbers.

Standardizing before a distance

Hands-on 11 · A · putting the four measurements on equal footing

K-means measures distance between rows, and a distance treats every column as equally important. StandardScaler rescales every column to mean 0 and standard deviation 1, so a column with a wider range in centimeters does not dominate for no reason connected to which flowers are actually similar.

Interpretation The first three rows, all setosa, now read as small numbers of both signs instead of centimeters — every column contributes on the same scale to any distance computed from here on.

Clustering with K-means

Hands-on 11 · A · one command

The command KMeans(n_clusters=3).fit(X_scaled) runs the loop Chapter 9 built by hand — assign, recompute, repeat — and stops when nothing moves. Three clusters are asked for, to match the three known species; the method itself has no way to know there are three.

Interpretation cluster_centers_ gives the position of each of the three centroids, in the standardized units — the average flower of each cluster K-means settled on.

Checking the clusters against the species

Hands-on 11 · A · a label the method never used

K-means never saw the word setosa, versicolor or virginica. pd.crosstab lines the cluster numbers up against the true species, kept aside since the first slide of this part, as a check.

Interpretation

  • One cluster is pure setosa and nothing else — the clearest structure in the data.
  • The other two clusters mostly separate versicolor from virginica, with a real number of flowers landing in the other species’ cluster — the two species overlap in these four measurements.
  • The agreement comes entirely from the four numbers; nothing here was told which species is which.

Hierarchical clustering, on a small sample

Hands-on 11 · B · a dendrogram needs room to draw

Hierarchical clustering compares every pair of clusters at every step, which makes a dendrogram of all 150 flowers too dense to read. groupby("species").head(5) takes the first five rows of each species — 15 flowers, small enough to draw every merge.

Interpretation Five setosa, five versicolor, five virginica, standardized the same way as the full dataset. The species column stays only to read the tree afterward — SciPy’s clustering never sees it.

The dendrogram

Hands-on 11 · B · SciPy’s linkage() and dendrogram()

linkage(X, method="average") runs the merge-the-closest-pair loop Chapter 9 built by hand, and dendrogram() draws the resulting tree in one more command.

Interpretation The five setosa leaves merge into one tight branch, well separated from the rest. Versicolor and virginica leaves interleave on the other branch — the same overlap the K-means crosstab found.

Cutting the tree two ways

Hands-on 11 · B · a distance, or a cluster count

Two SciPy calls fcluster(log, t=4.0, criterion="distance") cuts at a threshold height. fcluster(log, t=3, criterion="maxclust") instead asks directly for a number of clusters, and SciPy finds the height that gives it.

Interpretation Cutting for 3 clusters recovers setosa cleanly, but does not split versicolor from virginica the way the species label would: the two species land mostly, but not entirely, in one shared cluster on this small sample — a real result, not a rounding error, and a reminder that a clustering method answers a geometric question, not a biological one.

Association rules — the grocery transactions

Hands-on 11 · C · the same idea, a library-sized job

Chapter 9 hand-computed support, confidence and lift for one rule on eight baskets. Twelve baskets over five items — bread, butter, milk, eggs, coffee — are still small enough to read, but large enough that checking every rule by hand would be tedious. That is exactly the job a few lines of code do well.

Interpretation support() is the one function every rule below is built from: the share of baskets holding a given set of items, whatever its size.

Every rule between two items

Hands-on 11 · C · support, confidence and lift, for every pair

itertools.permutations lists every ordered pair of the five items — ordered, because confidence(bread → butter) and confidence(butter → bread) are generally different — and support, confidence and lift are computed for each.

Interpretation 20 rules — one for every ordered pair of the 5 items. Most describe a weak or accidental pattern; the next slide keeps only the ones worth reading.

The rules worth reading

Hands-on 11 · C · filtered by support and confidence

A minimum support of 0.25 and a minimum confidence of 0.6 keep only the rules that are both common enough to matter and reliable enough to act on, sorted by lift.

Interpretation

  • bread ↔︎ butter clears the bar in both directions, with lift just above 1 — a real, if modest, association.
  • eggs → butter and eggs → bread also clear the confidence bar; eggs → bread has lift just under 1, meaning bread is, if anything, not more likely once eggs are in the basket.
  • Rules built from milk and coffee are missing entirely — every one of them falls short on support, confidence, or both.

Exercise 1 — a new flower’s cluster

Hands-on 11 · D · K-means predicts, it does not just fit

A new flower measures 5.8 cm sepal length, 2.7 cm sepal width, 4.1 cm petal length. Fill the blank with its petal width, 1.0 cm, standardize it with the same scaler, and ask the fitted K-means model which cluster it belongs to.

The four columns are, in order, sepal length, sepal width, petal length, petal width. The blank is the fourth value, in centimeters.

new_flower = pd.DataFrame([[5.8, 2.7, 4.1, 1.0]], columns=flowers.columns)
kmeans.predict(scaler.transform(new_flower))[0]

The predicted cluster is the same one the crosstab slide showed mixing versicolor and virginica — this new flower’s measurements sit in that overlap, not with the clearly separated setosa cluster.

Exercise 2 — cutting for two clusters instead of three

Hands-on 11 · D · a coarser cut of the same tree

Fill the blank so the tree is cut for exactly two clusters instead of three, and report the two cluster sizes, largest first, as a list of two numbers.

criterion="maxclust" reads the blank as how many clusters are wanted, not a distance. Fill in 2.

clusters2 = fcluster(sample_log, t=2, criterion="maxclust")
sorted(pd.Series(clusters2).value_counts().tolist(), reverse=True)

[10, 5]: at two clusters, setosa (5 flowers) separates from versicolor and virginica together (10 flowers) — the one large gap in the dendrogram’s merge heights.

Exercise 3 — a rule the filter did not keep

Hands-on 11 · D · confidence below the bar

Fill in the two blanks to compute the confidence of milk → butter: the share of milk-baskets that also hold butter. Round to 3 decimals.

Confidence(X → Y) is support(X and Y) divided by support(X). Here X is milk, Y is butter: both blanks should read "butter" and "milk".

supp_milk_butter = support({"milk", "butter"}, groceries)
supp_milk = support({"milk"}, groceries)
round(supp_milk_butter / supp_milk, 3)

0.571: below the 0.6 confidence bar the filtered table used — common does not by itself mean reliable.

Exercise 4 — is it worth acting on?

Hands-on 11 · D · lift makes the call

Fill in the blank to compute the lift of coffee → milk, then decide: is this rule worth a business acting on?

Lift(X → Y) is confidence(X → Y) divided by support(Y). Y here is milk, so the blank is supp_milk.

lift = confidence / supp_milk
round(lift, 3)

1.143: lift above 1 says a real association, but support of only 2 of 12 baskets is too thin to act on – Chapter 9’s caution about apple and beer cuts both ways: a strong number on too little data is as unreliable as a weak one.

What to keep from the session

Hands-on 11 · D · takeaways

Three tools, three jobs KMeans(n_clusters=k).fit(X), linkage(X, method=...) with fcluster, and a five-line support() function reused across every pair of items — the same three ideas Chapter 9 built by hand, run on data too large to check by hand.

Standardize before a distance Every distance-based method here — K-means and hierarchical clustering both — needs its features on the same scale first, or the column with the widest range quietly decides the answer.

A clustering answers a geometric question Checking K-means and hierarchical clustering against the true species showed real agreement, but not a perfect match — a method that never saw the label cannot be expected to reproduce it exactly.
