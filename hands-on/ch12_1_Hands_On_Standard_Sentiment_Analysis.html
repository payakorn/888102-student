The session in four parts

Hands-on 12.1 · roadmap

The lecture walked one review through the workflow by hand. This session hands the same steps to two tools: CountVectorizer from scikit-learn prepares and counts the text, and SentimentIntensityAnalyzer from the VADER package carries the lexicon and its rules.

A · From text to numbersThirty reviews as a table; one review cut into tokens with and without stop words; the bag of words; the most frequent words, drawn.
B · A lexicon and its rulesThe lexicon opened and looked up; one sentence scored; the rules for not, really and but at work; all thirty reviews labeled.
C · ExercisesFour short graded exercises, each with a hint and a solution.
D · Where this leaves usOne label per review. 12.2 asks what the label hides.

Two tools CountVectorizer(stop_words="english") lowercases, drops punctuation, removes stop words and counts — steps 2 and 3 of the workflow and the bag of words, in one object. SentimentIntensityAnalyzer().polarity_scores(text) looks every word up, applies the rules and returns the compound score — steps 4 and 5. Everything else on these slides is preparing the data and reading the result.

The thirty reviews

Hands-on 12.1 · A · the data

Thirty short reviews of a noodle shop that takes orders through an app and delivers. Each carries the customer’s star rating, 1 to 5, which is kept to one side for now: the text is what this session works on.

Interpretation Thirty rows, two columns — and the column that matters is text. Nothing in it can be summed or averaged yet. Reviews 1 to 15 were rated 4 or 5 stars, 16 to 30 were rated 1 or 2; that is a fact about the data, not something the code has been told.

Tokenization and stop words

Hands-on 12.1 · A · steps 2 and 3 · one review, cut into words

A CountVectorizer carries an analyzer: the function that lowercases a text, drops the punctuation and cuts it into tokens. With stop_words="english" the analyzer also removes the common words that carry no opinion. Review 16 is put through both.

Interpretation

  • Ten tokens come out of the sentence: lowercase, the final period gone, one word per token — the table on the lecture slide, produced by the tool.
  • Five remain after filtering. The, and, were and two are on scikit-learn’s English stop-word list; delivery, took, hours, noodles and cold are the words that say something.
  • The opinion is still in there — cold — but nothing has yet said whether cold is good or bad. That is Part B’s job.

The bag of words

Hands-on 12.1 · A · text becomes a table of counts

fit_transform runs the analyzer over all thirty reviews, collects every distinct word as a column, and counts how many times each word appears in each review. The result is the bag of words: one row per review, one column per word, a number in every cell.

Interpretation

  • 30 rows, 89 columns. Thirty reviews use 89 distinct words once the stop words are gone. Each review is now a row of 89 numbers — data of the kind every earlier chapter worked with.
  • Review 1 has noodles and delicious; review 16 has noodles, delivery and cold; review 30 has noodles, delicious, delivery and late. The order of the words is gone — a bag, not a sentence — but which words were used is kept.

Try it Replace the five column names with counts.columns[:12] to see the first twelve words of the vocabulary, or remove stop_words="english" and run the cell again to watch the column count grow.

The most frequent words

Hands-on 12.1 · A · what the reviews talk about

Summing each column gives the number of times a word appears across all thirty reviews. Sorted and drawn, the top of the list says what the customers write about most.

Interpretation

  • Delivery, price, order, app, food, service lead the list. These are the aspects of the shop the customers write about — the parts 12.2 scores one by one.
  • Late appears four times, and every one of them is in a review rated 1 or 2 stars. A count alone hints at the problem; the sentiment scores of Part B make it explicit.

A sentiment lexicon

Hands-on 12.1 · B · step 4 · a list of words with a score each

A lexicon is a list of words with a sentiment score for each. VADER’s lexicon holds about 7,500 English words, rated by people on a scale from −4 (most negative) to +4 (most positive). The SentimentIntensityAnalyzer object carries the lexicon as a dictionary.

Interpretation

  • Delicious scores 2.7, good 1.9, okay 0.9: all positive, and the score says how strongly. Bad is −2.5, terrible −2.1, rude −2.0.
  • Cold, late and crashes are not in the lexicon. A lexicon knows only the words on its list. For a noodle shop, a cold bowl and a late driver are the two commonest complaints — and the lexicon has no score for either. 12.2 returns to this gap.

Try it Add words of your own to the list — "love", "worst", "noodles", "expensive" — and run again. A word with no opinion, or a word the raters never saw, comes back not in the lexicon.

One sentence, scored

Hands-on 12.1 · B · step 5 · standard sentiment analysis

polarity_scores reads a sentence, looks each word up in the lexicon, applies the rules, and returns one compound score between −1 and +1. Standard sentiment analysis turns that number into three labels: positive at or above 0.05, negative at or below −0.05, neutral in between.

Interpretation

  • compound 0.44 — positive, moderately. The other three numbers say what share of the sentence’s words were negative, neutral and positive: good is the only word with an opinion, so pos is about half and neg is zero.
  • sentiment_of is the whole classifier of standard sentiment analysis: one lookup, two thresholds, three labels. It is used on every sentence from here on.

The rules: not, really, but

Hands-on 12.1 · B · rule-based sentiment analysis

A lexicon alone would give The food was not good the same score as The food was good: both contain good. VADER adds rules — a negation flips the score, but gives more weight to what follows it, an intensifier such as really or an exclamation mark strengthens it. The lecture’s four sentences, scored by the tool:

Interpretation

  • not good scores −0.34 where good scored 0.44: the negation rule flipped the sign.
  • really good! scores 0.54: the intensifier and the exclamation mark each push the score further from zero.
  • good, but … really bad scores −0.64: the but rule gives the second half of the sentence more weight, so the sentence as a whole is negative even though it contains a positive word.

Try it Add "Although the food was good, the service was terrible." to the list. It scores only −0.05: VADER has a rule for but and none for although, so the two halves nearly cancel. Every rule list has gaps.

All thirty reviews, labeled

Hands-on 12.1 · B · the end of step 5

The function is applied to every review, and the compound score is kept alongside the label. Five reviews are shown with their star rating beside the label.

Interpretation

  • 15 positive, 13 negative, 2 neutral. Against the star ratings, the lexicon agrees on 28 of the 30 reviews.
  • The two neutrals are reviews 16 and 19 — two hours … cold and the app crashes. Their complaint words are not in the lexicon, so the compound score is exactly 0. The lexicon did not misjudge them; it did not see them.
  • Review 30 is negative at −0.65: the but rule outweighed delicious with disaster.

Try it Change the row numbers in the last line to see other reviews, or run reviews.sort_values("compound") to see all thirty from most negative to most positive.

Exercise 1 — the compound score

Hands-on 12.1 · C · reading polarity_scores

polarity_scores returns four numbers. Fill the blank with the name of the one that standard sentiment analysis reads, so that the box returns the score of the sentence.

The dictionary has four keys: "neg", "neu", "pos" and "compound". The label positive / neutral / negative is read from the one that combines them all.

analyzer.polarity_scores("The staff were rude and the soup was cold.")["compound"]

-0.4588 — negative. Rude is in the lexicon at −2.0; cold is not, so only one word set the score.

Exercise 2 — the stop-word list

Hands-on 12.1 · C · step 3 with the tool

Without stop words the thirty reviews have 89 distinct words. Fill the blank with the name of the built-in stop-word list, so that the box counts the words that remain.

scikit-learn has one built-in list, and it is named after the language the reviews are written in, in lowercase.

len(CountVectorizer(stop_words="english").fit(reviews["review"]).get_feature_names_out())

89. Remove the stop_words= argument altogether and the count rises to every distinct word in the thirty reviews, stop words included.

Exercise 3 — counting the negative reviews

Hands-on 12.1 · C · the value of a label

The sentiment column holds the label of every review. Fill the blank so the box counts how many reviews the lexicon labeled negative.

The three labels in the column are "positive", "neutral" and "negative", in lowercase. The comparison makes a column of True/False, and .sum() counts the Trues.

(reviews["sentiment"] == "negative").sum()

13. Change the word to "neutral" to count the two reviews whose complaint words the lexicon does not know.

Exercise 4 — the negation rule

Hands-on 12.1 · C · one word changes the label

The food was good is positive. Fill the blank with one word that makes the same sentence come out negative.

The rules slide had a sentence that scored −0.34 with exactly this shape. The word in it was a negation.

sentiment_of("The food was not good.")

'negative'. never works as well. Try "very" in the blank instead: the intensifier makes the sentence more positive, not less.

What to keep from the session

Hands-on 12.1 · D · takeaways

Text becomes a table by counting CountVectorizer lowercases, drops punctuation, removes stop words and counts: thirty reviews became 30 rows by 89 words. From there, every earlier chapter’s tools apply.

A lexicon plus rules reads opinion polarity_scores gives a compound score; the rules for not, really and but correct it; two thresholds in sentiment_of turn it into three labels.

A lexicon knows only its list Cold, late and crashes have no score, so two plain complaints came out neutral. The lexicon did not misjudge them; it did not see them.

Take-home The Chapter 12 notebook for Colab carries the same steps with a graded homework on a second set of reviews. Work there keeps; this page forgets when it is reloaded.

Where this leaves us Every review has one label and one compound score. 12.2 asks what the label hides — how strong, which emotion, which aspect — and turns the answers into a decision for the shop.
