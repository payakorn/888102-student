Fine-grained sentiment analysis

Hands-on 12.2 · A · five levels from the same score

Fine-grained sentiment analysis uses the same compound score but cuts it into five levels instead of three, with ±0.5 separating very positive and very negative from the rest. pd.cut does the cutting: the bins are the edges, the labels the names between them.

Interpretation

  • 9 very positive, 6 positive, 2 neutral, 7 negative, 6 very negative — the lecture’s table, computed. The standard labels said 15 and 13; the fine cut says most of the praise is strong and the complaints are split between mild and furious.
  • Review 1 (0.81) is very positive with two scored words; review 3 (0.49) stays merely positive because quick is not in the lexicon; review 17 (−0.66) is very negative with terrible and ignored.

Try it Move the inner edges from ±0.05 to ±0.2 and run again: the two neutrals become several, because mildly worded reviews now fall in the middle band. Where the edges sit is a decision, not a fact.

Emotion detection

Hands-on 12.2 · A · which emotion, not only which sign

Emotion detection asks a finer question than positive or negative: does the text show happiness, anger, frustration? The simplest tool is again a lexicon — but one that maps words to an emotion instead of a score. A small one is written here by hand.

Interpretation

  • Review 12 (Love the new app) shows happiness; 17 (Terrible service, the staff ignored us) shows anger; 19 (the app crashes every time) shows frustration.
  • Review 27 shows both anger and frustration — rude and late again. A review can carry more than one emotion, and a shop reads an angry customer and a frustrated one differently.
  • Real emotion lexicons hold thousands of words per emotion; this one holds six, and it works only because the words were chosen from these reviews.

Aspect-based sentiment analysis

Hands-on 12.2 · B · one review, several opinions

Review 30 is negative as a whole, yet it praises the noodles. Aspect-based sentiment analysis splits a review into clauses, names the aspect each clause is about — food, price, service, delivery, the app — and scores each clause on its own.

Interpretation

  • The noodles were delicious → food, positive. the delivery was a disaster → delivery, negative. The one review now carries two opinions, one per aspect, and the shop knows what to keep and what to fix.
  • two hours late → delivery, but neutral: late names the aspect, yet it has no score in the lexicon.
  • The aspect list is written by hand, one list of key words per aspect, for this shop. A hotel would list room, pool, breakfast; a software company interface, speed, support.

Complaints by aspect

Hands-on 12.2 · B · step 6 · from labels to a business insight

Every review is cut into clauses, every clause is given an aspect and a label, and the negative clauses are counted per aspect. That count is the interpretation step of the workflow: it says where the shop is losing customers.

Interpretation

  • Delivery draws 5 negative clauses, service, price and food 3 each, the app 1. Delivery was also the most frequent word in 12.1 — customers talk about it most and like it least.
  • The insight for the shop: the food is mostly praised; the delivery is where the complaints concentrate. Fix the delivery — more drivers, better packaging, a phone line that answers — before changing the recipe.
  • The table above the chart keeps the positive clauses too: delivery has 2, food 4. A count of complaints alone would hide that the food is doing well.

Reading the result Thirty reviews are a small sample, and two complaints about delivery were missed because cold and late are not in the lexicon. The direction of the insight is safe; the exact counts are not. Real reviews arrive by the thousand, and there the counts settle.

A classifier that learns sentiment

Hands-on 12.2 · C · machine-learning-based sentiment analysis

The lexicon was written by people, and it has no entry for cold, late or crashes. The other route is Chapter 10’s: labeled data — reviews whose sentiment is already known — a training step, and a prediction on new text. The star ratings supply the labels; the bag of words supplies the features.

Interpretation

  • One fit, then predict — the same two commands as the decision tree of Chapter 10.1, with text in place of a table of features. The pipeline turns each text into its bag of words and hands the counts to the classifier.
  • The app crashes and the order was late is negative for the model and neutral for the lexicon. The model learned from reviews 19, 24, 27, 29 and 30 that crashes and late go with 1 and 2 stars; the lexicon was never told.
  • What it learned is only what the thirty reviews contain. A complaint written in words that appear in none of them would leave the model guessing.

The words the model learned

Hands-on 12.2 · C · a lexicon nobody wrote

Inside the classifier sits a count: how many times each word appeared in negative reviews and how many times in positive ones. That count is what predict weighs. Listed for the words used at least three times, it is a lexicon — built from the labels instead of by hand.

Interpretation

  • Late: 4 negative, 0 positive — the strongest negative word the model has, and one the human lexicon does not know. Fair: 0 negative, 3 positive is its clearest positive word.
  • Delivery leans negative (4 to 2) and price leans positive (2 to 4) although neither word is an opinion. The model has learned this shop’s pattern — complaints are about delivery, praise is about price — not the meaning of the words. A different shop would teach it the opposite.
  • How good is the model? Chapter 10.2’s answer applies unchanged: hold back reviews it has not seen and compare its predictions with their stars. Thirty reviews are too few for that score to mean much, which is why this session reports none.

Word embeddings, in one paragraph In the bag of words every word is a column of its own, so delicious and tasty are as unrelated as delicious and late. A word embedding instead represents each word by a short list of numbers, chosen so that words used in similar sentences get similar numbers. Modern systems are built on that idea; how the numbers are found is beyond this course.

What to keep from the session

Hands-on 12.2 · takeaways

Four questions of one score pd.cut turns the compound score into five levels; an emotion lexicon swaps scores for feelings; scoring each clause gives one label per aspect. Same lexicon, different question each time.

Aspects turn labels into a decision Counted by aspect, the thirty reviews say: delivery 5 complaints, everything else 3 or fewer. Fix the delivery first. That sentence is what the whole workflow was for.

Or a classifier learns the words With labels, sentiment is Chapter 10’s problem again: fit on rated reviews, predict on new ones. The model learned late and cold, which no lexicon had — and only because the reviews contained them.

Take-home The Chapter 12 notebook for Colab carries the same steps with a graded homework on a second set of reviews. Work there keeps; this page forgets when it is reloaded.

Where this leaves us Chapter 12 closes the course. A tree, a line, a cluster and now a sentiment label all came from the same shape of work: data in a table, a model fitted to it, a result read back as a decision. Text was the last kind of data that had to be turned into a table first.
